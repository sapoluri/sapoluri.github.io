Part 1 · Foundations

You are already a state estimator

PART 1 OF 6  ·  STATE · MEASUREMENT · PREDICTION · THE BLEND

1  ·  The state is hidden. It always is.

Engineers use the word state for “the thing you actually care about, right now.” The position of a drone. The temperature inside a battery. Your speed, heading, and whether that pedestrian is about to step out.

The cruel bit: you almost never get to measure the state directly. You get clues.

STATE — what you want
“The ball is at x = 42 cm, moving at 3 m/s.”

Clean. Exact. Unavailable.
MEASUREMENT — what you get
“My eyes / GPS / encoder says 47 cm… give or take a lot.”

Noisy. Late. Sometimes flat-out wrong.

GPS jumps around a car park even when you're parked. A wheel encoder says you travelled 10 m — on a slippery floor, maybe you travelled 7. A camera sees a person in three frames, loses them behind a pillar, and sees them again. Estimation is what you do between the clues.

Estimation isn't about knowing. It's about carrying a good guess — and knowing how good it is.

That second half matters. A guess without an “I'm ± this much” attached is just confidence. A guess with uncertainty is something a system can act on safely: slow down when unsure, speed up when sure.

2  ·  Your belief is a blob, not a dot

Stop picturing your estimate as a single point. Picture it as a blob: a centre (your best guess) and a width (your uncertainty). Every prediction makes the blob wider — the world moves while you're not looking. Every decent measurement makes it narrower.

The memorable pattern: predict, predict, predict → big, vague blob. Measure → small, sharp blob. Every filter in this series — Kalman, particle, graph-based — is just a fancier way of doing grow, shrink, grow, shrink without losing track.

3  ·  The whole game is one dial: trust

When a new measurement arrives, you have two opinions:

① Your prediction — “based on everything so far, it should be here.”
② The new measurement — “the sensor says it's there.”

They disagree. They always disagree. So you blend:

new estimate = prediction  +  K × ( measurement − prediction )
K is your measurement trust dial · K = 0 → I don't trust the sensor measurement at all · K = 1 → irrespective of prediction, believe the sensor completely

That little ( measurement − prediction ) has a name you'll meet again in Part 2: the innovation, or residual — “how surprised am I?” Big surprise + high trust = big correction. Big surprise + low trust = “nice try, sensor, I barely moved.”

And here's the secret the rest of the series will unpack: a Kalman filter is just a machine that turns this dial automatically, based on how uncertain you are versus how noisy the sensor is. If you're very unsure and the sensor is good, K goes up. If you're confident and the sensor is junk, K goes down. That's it. That's the famous filter, in one sentence.

4  ·  The humble average was a filter all along

Before fancy filters, there was the moving average: “average the last 8 readings.” It works — noise cancels out — until the thing you're tracking actually moves. Then the average is always describing where it was. In Playground 2, watch the grey line: beautifully smooth, permanently behind on every turn.

The fix is to weight recent readings more. That's exponential smoothing — and look closely, it's exactly our blend formula with a fixed K:

estimate ← estimate + K × ( new reading − estimate )
Same equation. K here is often called α (alpha). Part 2 just learns how to choose it optimally, every step.

5  ·  The loop, on a postcard

1 · PREDICT

Use your model of how the world moves. Guess forwards. Admit more uncertainty.

2 · MEASURE

Get a clue. It will be noisy, late, or partial. That's normal — work with it.

3 · BLEND

Compare clue vs. prediction. Correct by K × surprise. Shrink uncertainty. Repeat — forever.

Filters (Parts 2–4) run this loop forwards, one step at a time, forgetting as they go. Graph-based methods (Part 5) do something bolder: they keep every prediction and measurement as a node in a big graph and re-solve the whole history when new evidence arrives — like a detective re-pinning an entire evidence board, not just the last photo. Same loop, different memory.

FILTERS = low memory, real-time, “now”GRAPH / SMOOTHING = full memory, revises the past

Part 2 · The Workhorse

The Kalman Filter — the dial that turns itself

PART 2 OF 6  ·  GAUSSIANS · PREDICT / UPDATE · KALMAN GAIN · Q & R

JULY 1969 · ON THE WAY TO THE MOON

Apollo's computer had to know where the spacecraft was — with a gyroscope that drifted and star sightings that were few, noisy, and late.

It couldn't afford to trust either one blindly. So, hundreds of times, it did what you did catching that ball in Part 1: predict from physics, peek at a measurement, blend by exactly the right amount. The maths for that “exactly right amount” had been published in 1960 by Rudolf Kálmán — and a version of it flew to the Moon. It has been running, in some form, in almost everything that moves ever since.

1  ·  What Part 1 left undone

In Part 1 you turned the trust dial K by hand. It worked — until the noise changed, the target sped up, or you looked away for five steps. Then your carefully chosen K was wrong, and you had no principled way to fix it.

The Kalman filter fixes exactly that, and nothing more mysterious than that. It keeps two numbers instead of one — and the easiest way to see them is to read the blob itself:

Hold x̂ fixed and change only P, and you get two completely different filters — same guess, opposite behaviour when the next measurement arrives:

Small P — “I'm confident”

P = 9  ·  σ = 3

Tall, narrow blob. Most of the belief sits within a few units of 42. Next measurement? Barely moves you — K will be small, because you already think you know.

Large P — “I'm guessing”

P = 225  ·  σ = 15

Low, wide blob. 42 is still the best single guess, but 30 or 55 are nearly as plausible. Next measurement? Moves you a lot — K will be large, because you were mostly guessing.

Same x̂, different P, opposite reaction to the same sensor reading. That contrast — invisible if you only carry the dot — is exactly why the filter carries both numbers.

Because once you carry P, you no longer have to guess K. You can compute it, fresh, at every single step, from two honest questions: how unsure am I? and how noisy is this sensor?

If you're very unsure and the sensor is good, lean on the sensor. If you're confident and the sensor is junk, barely move. The Kalman filter is that sentence, made precise.

2  ·  The five equations — now you can play with them

Textbooks print five equations and scare people off. Don't just read these — poke them. Grouped, there are just the two moves you already know: predict (blob drifts and grows) and update (blob gets pulled and shrinks). The cards below are the map. The Equation Lab right underneath is the territory — every slider moves a real number inside the equations, and the blobs obey instantly.

① PREDICT — “where should it be by now?”

x̂⁻ = F · x̂ + B · u
P⁻  = F · P · Fᵀ + Q

Roll the guess forward with your motion model (F). Roll the uncertainty forward too — then add Q, the process noise: the honest admission that the world doesn't follow your model exactly. No measurement yet, so P can only grow.

② UPDATE — “a clue arrived, blend it”

K  = P⁻Hᵀ / ( H·P⁻Hᵀ + R )
x̂ = x̂⁻ + K · ( z − H·x̂⁻ )
P  = ( I − K·H ) · P⁻

R is the measurement noise — how much this sensor typically lies. z − H·x̂⁻ is the innovation from Part 1: your surprise. K weights the surprise, the guess moves, and P shrinks.

In the 1D, position-only case the gain collapses to the formula you secretly used in Playground 1:

K  =  P⁻  /  ( P⁻ + R )
Your uncertainty ÷ (your uncertainty + sensor noise) · P ≫ R → K → 1, trust the sensor · P ≪ R → K → 0, trust yourself

Read that fraction slowly, because it's the entire filter in miniature. K is your share of the total doubt. If almost all the doubt is yours, the measurement should move you a lot. If almost all the doubt is the sensor's, it should barely move you at all. No hand-tuning — the ratio does it, every step, as P breathes.

x̂, P — current belief→ PREDICT: +Q, P grows→ MEASURE: z arrives→ K = P⁻/(P⁻+R)→ UPDATE: P shrinks→ repeat, forever

Try the extremes: set P tiny and R huge — K collapses toward 0 and even a measurement at 90 barely nudges you from 42. Now flip them — huge P, tiny R — K rushes toward 1 and you land almost exactly on the sensor. Same equations, opposite personalities. You chose the personality with two sliders.

Three experiments that make §2 stick: ① Make K = 0.50 exactly — can you? (Hint: K is ½ when P⁻ = R, whatever the actual values are.) ② Keep K fixed, double the surprise — slide z twice as far from 42 and watch x̂ move twice as far, but P shrink by exactly the same amount. Surprise moves the centre; only the ratio P⁻∶R decides the shrink. ③ Predict without peeking — crank Q alone: P⁻ balloons, so the next measurement will hit harder. Q doesn't move you now — it makes you more movable later.

Start: you begin at 20, truth starts at 20 too — but truth moves at a steady +2 per step and your velocity guess will never be perfect. Predict a few times without updating and watch P (and your error) grow. Then update and watch K spend that uncertainty.

Two things to burn in. First: K is not a tuning knob you set once — in a healthy filter it wobbles step to step as P grows and shrinks, settling into a rhythm once the breathing is regular (the “steady state”). Second: after a long run of predicts with no measurement, P is huge, so the next K is close to 1 — the filter quite rightly says “I've been blind, believe the sensor.” That behaviour falls out of the fraction. Nobody programmed it in.

4  ·  Q and R — the two knobs you actually tune

If K is automatic, what's left for the engineer? Two honest numbers, and they are a story you tell the filter about the world:

KnobMeansSet it too low →Set it too high →
Q — process noise“How much do I distrust my motion model per step?”Filter gets smug. P stays tiny, K stays low, and when the target manoeuvres the filter lags and can't catch up.Filter gets anxious. P inflates every step, K stays high, estimate jitters after every measurement.
R — measurement noise“How much do I distrust this sensor?” (ideally from its datasheet / calibration)Filter believes a noisy sensor too much — jittery, chases outliers.Filter ignores a good sensor — smooth but slow, wastes information.

The classic beginner mistake is tuning Q and R by vibes until the plot “looks nice” on one recording — then watching it fall apart on the next. The discipline is: R comes from the sensor (measure it standing still; the scatter is R), and Q comes from the manoeuvre (how violently can the thing you're tracking actually change its motion?). Tune the story, not the plot.

6  ·  When Kalman is exactly right — and when it isn't

Under its assumptions, the Kalman filter isn't just good, it's provably optimal: no other estimator can squeeze more out of the same information. The catch is the assumptions, and you should be able to recite them like a pre-flight checklist:

✓ IT ASSUMES
• Motion & measurement models are linear (straight-line maths)
• All noise is Gaussian — bell-shaped, no wild outliers
• You know Q and R (roughly)
• One blob is enough to describe your belief
✕ REALITY, OFTEN
• Robots turn — headings wrap, geometry curves → Part 3: EKF / UKF
• A lost robot could be in two corridors at once — two blobs → Part 4: particles
• Sensors glitch, drop out, and lie — gating & robust tricks patch this, up to a point

Notice the glitch button in Playground 4: one outlier yanks even a well-tuned Kalman filter, because a Gaussian sensor model says wild readings are merely unlikely, never impossible — so the filter dutifully moves part-way toward nonsense. Real systems add an innovation gate: if the surprise is absurdly large compared with P + R, reject the measurement outright. Knowing why that patch exists is worth more than the patch.

Predict: grow P by Q  ·  Gain: K = P⁻/(P⁻+R)  ·  Update: shrink P by (1−K)
The whole of Part 2 on one line. If you remember the breathing — grow, shrink, grow, shrink — you remember the Kalman filter.
